
# Call Recovery

## Purpose

Use this guide when the caller:

- loses momentum,
- over-explains,
- gets interrupted,
- sounds too sales-oriented,
- asks for too much too soon,
- becomes unsure how to continue,
- realizes they made an unsupported statement.

The goal is to recover naturally without making the mistake larger.

---

## Core Recovery Principle

Do not panic.

Do not restart the entire call.

A simple reset is usually enough.

Useful phrases:

"Let me simplify that."

"The short version is..."

"Let me give you the quick context."

"I probably gave you more detail than you needed there."

"Let me explain why I actually called."

Use these sparingly.

---

## Recovery From Over-Explaining

### Problem

The caller gives too much detail before the prospect speaks.

### Recovery

"Let me pause there because I just gave you quite a bit."

"Are you already familiar with Frost & Sullivan and what we do?"

Then adjust based on the answer.

### Coaching Rule

Do not keep talking because silence feels uncomfortable.

Create dialogue.

---

## Recovery From Sounding Too Sales-Oriented

### Problem

The caller uses promotional language.

Examples:

"This is a great opportunity."

"We'd love to give you an award."

### Recovery

"Let me clarify that because I don't want to make it sound like a sales pitch."

"The reason we're reaching out is that [COMPANY] surfaced during [ANALYST_NAME]'s market research."

"The Best Practices nomination came out of that research."

Then continue.

---

## Recovery From Saying Award Too Early

### Problem

The prospect becomes skeptical because the call sounds promotional.

### Recovery

"That's fair."

"The recognition is really the result of the research process."

"The reason for my call is to give you the context around the evaluation and explain why the company came to the analyst's attention."

---

## Recovery From Getting Interrupted

### Prospect

"I only have 30 seconds. What's this about?"

### Response

"Absolutely."

"I'm calling on behalf of [ANALYST_NAME], who's leading research in the [INDUSTRY] space."

"[COMPANY] came to our attention during that work and has been nominated for evaluation through our Best Practices Recognition program."

"I'm really just trying to find 15 minutes at a better time to give you the full context."

Then stop talking.

---

## Recovery From Losing the Thread

### Three-Point Reset

Return to:

1. Why Frost & Sullivan is calling.
2. Why the company is relevant.
3. What the next step is.

Example:

"The short version is that [ANALYST_NAME] is researching the [INDUSTRY] space, [COMPANY] came to our attention during that work, and I'm trying to find 15 minutes to explain the process and answer any questions."

---

## Recovery From a Weak Meeting Ask

### Weak

"Would you maybe want to learn more?"

### Recovery

"Let me be more specific."

"I'd like to find 15 to 20 minutes so I can give you the full overview, explain the evaluation process, and answer any questions."

Then:

"I can send you my Microsoft Bookings link so you can choose whatever time works best."

---

## Recovery From Asking for Too Much Time

### Problem

The caller asks a lukewarm contact for 60 minutes.

### Recovery

"Actually, I don't want to overcomplicate it."

"For the initial conversation, 15 to 20 minutes is plenty."

"The full analyst briefing only makes sense later if it's appropriate."

This reduces pressure and restores credibility.

---

## Recovery From an Unsupported Claim

### Problem

The caller realizes they stated something they cannot confirm.

### Recovery

"I want to correct that."

"I don't want to give you the wrong information, so let me confirm the exact details rather than guess."

Do not double down.

Accuracy is more important than appearing certain.

---

## Recovery From an Incorrect Category or Criteria Statement

"Let me correct that because I don't want to misstate the specifics."

"The exact category and criteria can vary by evaluation, so I'd rather confirm what's applicable here."

---

## Recovery From Saying the Company Has Been Selected

### Problem

The caller accidentally implies final recognition.

### Recovery

"Let me clarify that."

"The company has been nominated for evaluation. That isn't the same as a final recognition decision."

Then continue with the research process.

---

## Recovery From a Defensive Objection Response

### Problem

The caller argues with the prospect.

### Recovery

"That's fair."

"I can understand why you'd ask."

Then return to factual context.

Example:

"The reason I make the distinction is that the outreach begins with the analyst's market research. The evaluation is separate from any later commercial discussion."

---

## Recovery From Send Me an Email

### Problem

The caller keeps pushing for the meeting.

### Recovery

"Absolutely. I'll send that over."

"I'll keep it concise and include my Microsoft Bookings link so you can choose a time if you'd like to continue the conversation."

Do not fight the request.

---

## Recovery From Gatekeeper Resistance

### Gatekeeper

"They're not available. Send something to the general inbox."

### Response

"Absolutely, I can do that."

"Since this is tied to [ANALYST_NAME]'s research, I just want to make sure it reaches the right person."

"Would you recommend Strategy, Product, Analyst Relations, Communications, or someone else?"

---

## Recovery From Wrong Contact

"No problem at all."

"Who would you recommend I speak with?"

If they do not know:

"Would that normally sit with Strategy, Product, Innovation, Marketing, Communications, or Analyst Relations?"

---

## Recovery From Silence

If the prospect goes quiet after a long explanation:

Do not keep filling the silence.

Use one question.

Examples:

"Does that make sense so far?"

"How familiar are you with Frost & Sullivan?"

"Would you be the right person for this?"

The goal is to restore two-way dialogue.

---

## Recovery From Prospect Skepticism

### Prospect

"What's the catch?"

### Response

"That's a fair question."

"The reason we're reaching out is that [COMPANY] surfaced through the analyst's market research."

"The evaluation process is separate from any later commercial discussion."

Then:

"I'm happy to walk you through how the process works so you can decide whether it makes sense to participate."

---

## Recovery From Talking Too Fast

If the caller notices they are rushing:

Pause.

Slow down.

Use a shorter sentence.

Example:

"The short version is pretty simple."

Then state the core message in one or two sentences.

---

## Recovery From Prospect Confusion

### Prospect

"I'm still not sure what you're asking me to do."

### Response

"Absolutely. The ask is simple."

"I'm looking to schedule a 15-to-20-minute conversation so I can explain the research context, walk you through the Best Practices process, and answer your questions."

---

## Recovery From Meeting Purpose Confusion

### Prospect

"Why do I need another call?"

### Response

"The reason for the short conversation is to give you the full context without trying to cover everything on an unexpected call."

"It also gives you a chance to ask questions and helps us make sure the analyst has an accurate understanding of the company."

---

## Recovery From Scheduling Friction

### Prospect

"I don't know what my schedule looks like."

### Response

"No problem."

"I'll send you my Microsoft Bookings link so you can choose whatever time works best once you have your calendar in front of you."

---

## Recovery From Multiple Objections

If the prospect gives repeated objections:

Do not keep overcoming endlessly.

After one or two genuine attempts:

"Understood. I don't want to keep pushing."

"I appreciate you taking the time."

If appropriate:

"I can send the information over and leave the door open if it becomes relevant later."

---

## Recovery From a Soft No

Do not immediately repeat the same meeting ask.

Use:

"Absolutely, I understand."

"Before I let you go, can I ask one quick question?"

"Is it mainly the timing, or are you still unsure what the conversation would cover?"

Address the actual concern.

Then make one second ask.

---

## Recovery From a Hard No

Do not recover into another pitch.

Use:

"Understood. I appreciate your time."

Then end professionally.

---

## Recovery From an Awkward Moment

If the caller stumbles over wording:

Do not apologize repeatedly.

Use:

"Let me say that more clearly."

Then restate the point simply.

Avoid:

"Sorry, I'm terrible at explaining this."

"Sorry, I messed that up."

---

## Recovery From Forgetting a Detail

If the caller does not know:

"I don't want to guess and give you the wrong information."

"Let me confirm that and follow up."

This is stronger than improvising.

---

## Recovery From Prospect Challenging the Analyst's Involvement

### Prospect

"Did the analyst actually ask you to call me?"

If true:

Answer accurately.

If not:

Do not imply that they did.

Preferred:

"I'm part of the research team supporting [ANALYST_NAME]'s project, and my role is to help coordinate the outreach and next conversation."

---

## Recovery Coaching Rule

A strong recovery should:

- acknowledge the moment,
- simplify,
- restore the research positioning,
- return to dialogue,
- avoid defensiveness,
- avoid inventing information,
- move toward the appropriate next step.

The goal is not perfection.

The goal is to regain clarity and credibility.
